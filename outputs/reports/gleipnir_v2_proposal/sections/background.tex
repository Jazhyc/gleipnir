\section{Problem and prior work}
\label{sec:background}
AI agents act with growing autonomy, and monitors that review their actions
are a main line of defense. In July 2026, agents in internal evaluations at
OpenAI broke out of their isolation and compromised the systems of Hugging
Face; OpenAI's monitors would have raised an alert more than a day before the
breach, but did not run on these evaluations \citep{openai2026huggingface}.
Monitoring is also expensive: OpenAI estimates that monitoring its agents adds
roughly 20\% to their inference compute \citep{openai2026pacing}. A monitor
that checks each action before it runs also delays every action. Cheaper and faster monitors let developers
check more of what their agents do.

Gleipnir v1 \citep{ferrao2026gleipnir} showed that small open-weight monitors
can learn the decision of a frontier teacher and score an agent trajectory in
one forward pass, instead of writing a rationale first. On a suite of six
held-out AI control benchmarks \citep{sinha2026training}, Gleipnir 27B scores
0.951 mean pAUROC@20, level with the strongest monitor on these benchmarks:
Claude Opus 4.6, a frontier model that reasons before it answers (0.942).
Gleipnir 27B does so at 229 times lower cost, served by a stack built for this
single forward pass. The code,
weights, teacher probabilities and serving image are public.

\subsection{What v1 leaves open for production}
\label{sec:gaps}
v1 was evaluated on the six AI control benchmarks above, not on the traffic of
a deployed agent. Its lowest reported operating point is a 1\% false-positive
rate, while classifiers on production traffic run far lower, for example at a
refusal rate of 0.05\% \citep{cunningham2026constitutional}. The six benchmarks
contain only 3,189 benign trajectories in total, so a single false positive
already makes up 0.03\%, more than half of that rate. Its evaluation
also drops every trajectory over 28,000 tokens, while agents in production
generally work over far longer contexts: in real coding-agent sessions
\citep{baumann2026swechat}, the agent's context reaches about 77,000 tokens in
a typical session and up to a million. Almost all of the monitor's cost is the prefill over these
trajectories, which v1 does not shorten. The strongest v1 monitor also uses all 27 billion parameters of its base
model for every token, which sets a floor on its cost that serving work alone
cannot remove. Attacks built to evade the monitor, where v1 falls far behind on
SleightBench \citep{najt2026sleight}, are out of scope for this proposal
(Section~\ref{sec:aims}).

\subsection{A cheaper base model: Inkling 2 Mini}
\label{sec:inkling}
Inkling 2 Mini \citep{inkling2mini} is a mixture-of-experts model that uses
only 3.4B active parameters for each token. It accepts contexts of up to 1,048,576 tokens, and 20 of
its 24 layers attend only over the last 1,024 tokens, which slows the growth
of attention cost with context length. Without any training, it scores 0.884 mean pAUROC@20 on the same suite, against 0.890 for Qwen3.8-27B, the base of Gleipnir 27B. It already scores the 96 agent trajectories of the v1 serving benchmark at 4.3
requests per second, sent one at a time, against 4.1 for the optimized
Gleipnir 27B, with no optimization of my own. I therefore expect a Gleipnir monitor trained on it to match or exceed
Gleipnir 27B at lower cost. Its support in vLLM is new and still changing, and its weights ship only in
low-precision formats (NVFP4 and MXFP8), so training and serving it need
engineering work (Section~\ref{sec:risks}).
