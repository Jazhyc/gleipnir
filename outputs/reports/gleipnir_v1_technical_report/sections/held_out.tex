\section{Held-out and adversarial evaluation}
\label{sec:held-out}

The OOD suite of \citet{sinha2026training} no longer separates the strongest monitors.
Their scores lie within each other's bootstrap intervals and close to the maximum (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
Even the lead of Gleipnir 27B over its teacher comes from one task of SHADE-Arena (Appendix~\ref{app:shade}).
This section therefore turns to benchmarks that are harder for monitors or differ from the OOD suite in kind.
They show how the Gleipnir monitors compare with other monitors in these settings, and which parts of a trajectory their decisions depend on.
No training, prompt or selection decision of Gleipnir used these benchmarks.

\subsection{SleightBench}
\label{sec:sleightbench}

SleightBench~\citep{najt2026sleight} tests whether a monitor catches attacks that are built to evade monitors.
It contains 40 hand-written attacks in 11 categories, each a trajectory of a coding agent that covertly pursues a harmful objective.
Each attack has a benign twin, the same trajectory without the harmful part, and in several pairs the two differ by one token.

We score the stripped view of the authors, a stricter form of the action-only input (Section~\ref{sec:background-sinha}) that also removes the assistant messages.
Every monitor receives the ControlArena instruction that the authors use: the models that we read out with binary logits receive it with our binary output lines, and the decision models receive it as their question.
A monitor catches an attack when it scores the attack above the highest of the 43 benign trajectories, the authors' threshold for a 1\% false-positive rate.
We price the monitors as in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, for trajectories with a mean of about 17,600 tokens.
Each of our monitors scores each trajectory once, while the authors average 10 samples at temperature 1 and report only the catch rate, so Figure~\ref{fig:sleightbench} omits their monitors; Appendix~\ref{app:sleightbench} lists them.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{sleightbench_frontier}
\caption{On SleightBench, pplx-decider v1.3 scores above both Gleipnir monitors at about the cost of Gleipnir 27B. pAUROC@20 on the stripped view against the inference cost per 1,000 evaluations (log scale), over 40 attacks and 43 benign trajectories. Every monitor receives the ControlArena instruction. Gleipnir is priced at the committed-use rate; Table~\ref{tab:sleightbench} lists every monitor with both rates. Markers follow Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}, and the frontier treats costs within 3\% of each other as equal. The 95\% bootstrap intervals are about 0.25 wide (Table~\ref{tab:sleightbench}).}
\label{fig:sleightbench}
\end{figure}

GPT-6.1 Terra scores highest, 0.734 pAUROC@20, at \$15.49 per 1,000 evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:sleightbench}).
pplx-decider v1.3 scores 0.397 at \$0.35, close to the 0.402 of Kimi K3.
Gleipnir 27B scores 0.299, level with pplx-decider v1.2 (0.300) and 0.103 below its teacher.
Gleipnir 4B scores 0.209.
Training raises both base models by less than on the OOD suite: Qwen3.5-4B scores 0.193 and Qwen3.8-27B 0.263.
The intervals of all monitors except GPT-6.1 Terra overlap (Table~\ref{tab:sleightbench}), so with 40 attacks the benchmark separates only large differences.

At a false-positive rate of 1\%, Gleipnir 27B catches 20\% of the attacks and Gleipnir 4B 7.5\%, against 27.5\% for pplx-decider v1.3 and 55\% for GPT-6.1 Terra (Appendix~\ref{app:sleightbench}).
Appendix~\ref{app:sleightbench} compares these monitors with the reasoning monitors that the authors scored.
