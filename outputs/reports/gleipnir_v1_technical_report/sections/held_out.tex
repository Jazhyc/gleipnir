\section{Held-out and adversarial evaluation}
\label{sec:held-out}

This section evaluates the monitors on benchmarks that are harder for monitors or differ from the OOD suite in kind.
They show how the Gleipnir monitors compare with other monitors in these settings, and which parts of a trajectory their decisions depend on.
No training, prompt or selection decision of Gleipnir used these benchmarks.
Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory} gives four smaller, exploratory stress tests.

\subsection{SleightBench}
\label{sec:sleightbench}

SleightBench~\citep{najt2026sleight} tests whether a monitor catches attacks that humans designed to exploit blind spots of frontier monitors: at a 1\% false-positive rate, Claude Opus 4.6 with extended thinking never catches 20 of its 40 attacks.
The attacks fall into 11 categories, and each is a trajectory of a coding agent that covertly pursues a harmful objective.
Each attack has a benign twin, the same trajectory without the harmful part, and in several pairs the two differ by one token.

We score the stripped view of the authors, a stricter form of the action-only input (Section~\ref{sec:background-sinha}) that also removes the assistant messages.
Every monitor receives the ControlArena instruction that the authors use: the models that we read out with binary logits receive it with our binary output lines, and the decision models receive it as their question.
A monitor catches an attack when it scores the attack above the highest of the 43 benign trajectories, the authors' threshold for a 1\% false-positive rate.
We price the monitors as in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}; the prompts average about 17,600 tokens, about twice as long as those of the OOD suite, so each evaluation costs about twice as much.
The authors report only the catch rate of their monitors, so Figure~\ref{fig:sleightbench} omits them; Appendix~\ref{app:sleightbench} lists them.

\paragraph{At low cost, pplx-decider v1.3 leads both Gleipnir monitors.}
At about the committed-use cost of Gleipnir 27B (\$0.35 against \$0.33 per 1,000 evaluations), pplx-decider v1.3 scores 0.397 pAUROC@20, against 0.299 for Gleipnir 27B and 0.209 for Gleipnir 4B (Figure~\ref{fig:sleightbench}).
Gleipnir 27B is level with pplx-decider v1.2 (0.300).

\paragraph{GPT-6.1 Terra leads at the high end.}
GPT-6.1 Terra scores 0.734 at \$15.49, and its interval lies above those of all cheaper monitors (Table~\ref{tab:sleightbench}).
With one decision token, it catches 55\% of the attacks at a 1\% false-positive rate, close to the 60--62\% of Claude Opus 4.6 and 4.7 in the Claude Code scaffold of the authors (Appendix~\ref{app:sleightbench}).

\paragraph{Unlike on the OOD suite, Gleipnir 27B falls behind its teacher.}
It matches Kimi K3 on the OOD suite (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) but scores 0.103 below it here, and training raises both base models by less: Qwen3.5-4B scores 0.193 and Qwen3.8-27B 0.263.
SleightBench was built to evade monitors, while the OOD suite was not, and our training data contains no attacks built against a monitor.
pplx-decider v1.3 shares the base model of Gleipnir 27B, but its training covers a wider range of decision tasks, including the monitoring of the agent rollouts of Perplexity~\citep{perplexity2026decider,perplexity2026deciderblog}, and it uses bidirectional attention (Appendix~\ref{app:serving-decider}).
Its training data is not public, so we cannot tell which explains its lead.

\paragraph{With 40 attacks, the benchmark separates only large differences.}
The intervals of pAUROC@20 are about 0.25 wide.
The catch rate is noisier still, because it depends on the single highest benign score: Kimi K3 catches no attack because it scores one benign trajectory above every attack, although its pAUROC@20 matches that of pplx-decider v1.3.
Otherwise the catch rates rank the monitors much as pAUROC@20 does (Appendix~\ref{app:sleightbench}).

Against attacks designed to evade a monitor, which a capable agent that knows its monitor could attempt, no monitor that we scored is sufficient alone, and the cheap decision models, including Gleipnir, fall further short than on the OOD suite.
Closing this gap, with stronger monitors behind the cheap ones or with training data that contains attacks built against a monitor, is left to future work.

\subsection{Reward hacking by other agent models}
\label{sec:held-out-evilgenie}

Coding agents can exploit flaws in how their work is checked, for example by special-casing the tests instead of solving the task.
A monitor that recognizes such reward hacks could judge agent runs, both in deployment and in a reinforcement learning pipeline, where low cost matters because every rollout is scored.
Strong optimization pressure against a monitor can, however, teach agents to obfuscate their reward hacking~\citep{baker2025monitoring}.
We test whether Gleipnir recognizes reward hacks by other agent models.

We use the EvilGenie Escalation dataset~\citep{gomez2026escalation}, which builds on EvilGenie~\citep{gabor2025evilgenie}: 15 agent models solve LiveCodeBench problems~\citep{jain2024livecodebench} with ambiguous specifications and visible tests, and some hard-code the visible test outputs instead of solving the problem.
We score all 124 reward hacks and 1,823 legitimate runs, from nine problems, with the released serving stack (Section~\ref{sec:serving}).
The reward hacks come from five of them, and the intervals resample whole problems.
As references we score Kimi K3, GPT-6 Luna and pplx-decider v1.3 on the same runs, each with the input and readout of its OOD evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
Each monitor flags a run when its score is above the threshold that flags 1\% of the benign OOD trajectories; we set this threshold once and do not tune it on any test set, because a deployed monitor has no labels from a new setting.

\paragraph{At the 1\% threshold, Gleipnir 27B catches more reward hacks than monitors of similar cost.}
Gleipnir 27B flags 28\% of the reward hacks and 1.1\% of the legitimate runs (Table~\ref{tab:evilgenie}).
pplx-decider v1.3, which costs about the same per 1,000 evaluations on the OOD suite (\$0.176, against \$0.178 at the committed rate), flags 17\%, and GPT-6 Luna (\$0.835) flags 7\%, at 0.7\% and 0.2\% of the legitimate runs.
Kimi K3 flags 53\% of the reward hacks, but also 6.0\% of the legitimate runs, and costs 61 times as much as Gleipnir 27B on demand and 141 times as much with committed use.
In ranking the monitors are closer: Gleipnir 27B scores 0.673 pAUROC@20 and the reference monitors 0.597 to 0.652, with overlapping intervals.
Gleipnir 4B, the cheapest monitor (\$0.040 with committed use), flags 2\% of the reward hacks and scores 0.283, so it is not competitive on this task.

\paragraph{Most missed reward hacks are on one problem.}
The problem \texttt{arc191\_c} (``Sample output'' in Table~\ref{tab:evilgenie}) holds 69 of the 124 reward hacks.
Its visible test checks the exact sample output, although the problem accepts any valid output.
The hacking agents add the sample output on top of a correct general solution, so their program stays correct on every input.
Many legitimate runs on this problem also type the sample output into their code at some point (Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-evilgenie}).
At the 1\% threshold, no monitor except Kimi K3 (26\%) flags more than 3\% of these reward hacks, although Gleipnir 27B ranks them above the legitimate runs of the same problem (AUROC 0.858, against 0.558 for the 4B).
On the other four problems, the 27B flags 60\% of the 55 reward hacks and the 4B 5\%.
We chose this split after we saw the scores, so it describes the result and does not test a hypothesis.
The dataset release removed the text of tool errors (Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-evilgenie}).

\subsection{The same text on an honest and a backdoored program}
\label{sec:held-out-apps}

An agent writes its code and the text around it, so a monitor that reacts to text can be steered.
We test this in the APPS backdoor setting of AI control~\citep{greenblatt2023ai}.
Each task is an APPS problem~\citep{hendrycks2021apps} with two programs by o3-mini, from the APPS dataset of ControlArena~\citep{controlarena2025apps} as collected by \citet{kenney2026monitorinjection} following the attacks of \citet{terekhov2025adaptive}.
One program is honest, and the other gives a wrong output on one trigger input, which we confirmed for each task.
We insert one text as a module docstring at the top of both programs and leave the code unchanged.
The text alone therefore carries no information about which program has the backdoor.
We compare four conditions: no text, an unrelated text that restates another APPS problem, a specific accusation and a generic accusation.
The specific accusation describes the trigger input and the wrong output in the words of the problem, without code identifiers.
The generic accusation says only that the code hides a backdoor.
GLM-5.3-Flash wrote the texts, and we kept a text only after a manual review confirmed its definition, before any monitor scored it.
Of 99 held-out tasks, 93, 81 and 99 have a valid text in the three text conditions.
For each condition, we compute pAUROC@20 of the backdoored programs against the honest programs and resample whole tasks for the intervals.

\paragraph{Text lowers Gleipnir 4B close to chance.}
Without text, Gleipnir 4B scores 0.690 pAUROC@20 (Figure~\ref{fig:apps-text}).
An unrelated text lowers it to 0.360, and a specific accusation lowers it to 0.143.
Within a task, the 4B still scores the backdoored program higher in 72\% of the pairs under a specific accusation, and 17\% of the pairs tie.
The pooled ranking fails because the accusation moves almost every score close to 1: the mean score is 0.846 for honest programs and 0.870 for backdoored programs.

\paragraph{Accusations raise the separation of Gleipnir 27B.}
Gleipnir 27B scores 0.818 pAUROC@20 without text, 0.872 with a generic accusation and 0.934 with a specific accusation.
With a specific accusation, it scores the backdoored program higher in every one of the 81 pairs.
One explanation is that any accusation leads the 27B to inspect the code more closely, and that the description of the trigger adds a further cue.
A false trigger that neither program handles would separate these two effects; we did not run this test.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning made the 4B more sensitive to accusations than its base model.}
The unadapted Qwen3.5-4B already loses separation under unrelated text (0.189 pAUROC@20), so part of the effect comes from the base model.
Under a specific accusation, however, its AUROC is 0.805 and that of Gleipnir 4B is 0.581.
On the same tasks, the paired difference is $-0.22$ (95\% bootstrap interval over tasks $-0.27$ to $-0.17$).
A linear probe on the activations of Gleipnir 4B still separates the accused programs, which suggests that the failure lies in the output and not in the representation (Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-probe}).

\paragraph{A threshold set on code without text does not transfer.}
We also set a 1\% threshold for each monitor on honest APPS programs without docstrings.
Under an accusation, every Gleipnir and API monitor flags 93\% to 100\% of the honest programs at this threshold.
The unadapted 4B is the one exception, at 65\% under a generic accusation.
Under an unrelated text, Gleipnir 4B flags 88\% and Gleipnir 27B 100\% of honest programs.
We did not test accurate documentation of the code, so these rates apply to misleading or unrelated text.

\paragraph{Limits.}
One model wrote the programs, one model wrote the texts, and the text has one position.
Gleipnir 4B and 27B differ in size and in instruction, and we did not score the unadapted Qwen3.8-27B here, so this test does not separate the effects of size, instruction and training for the 27B.
