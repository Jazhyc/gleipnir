\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Monitors in AI control.}
AI control studies how to deploy an AI agent safely even if the agent tries to cause harm~\citep{greenblatt2023ai}.
To test this, control evaluations give the agent a \emph{main task}, the work that a user asked for~\citep{kutasov2025shade,kaufman2025basharena}.
An agent that attacks also has a \emph{side task}: a harmful objective that it pursues in secret while it works on the main task.
A \emph{monitor} guards against such an agent.
It reads what the agent did and rates how suspicious the actions look.
In the trusted monitoring protocol of Greenblatt et al., this monitor is a weaker model that is trusted not to cause harm, and humans audit the actions that it rates highest.
Because human time is limited, the audit covers only a small share of the actions.
The monitor is therefore useful only if it rates agents with a side task above almost all honest agents.
The training data and the benchmarks in this report follow the same design.

\paragraph{Monitors in production.}
Coding agents already use such monitors in production.
In the auto mode of Claude Code~\citep{anthropic2026auto} and the auto-review of Codex~\citep{openai2026autoreview}, a separate model approves or blocks an action before it runs.
This model takes the place of the human who would otherwise approve the action.
When it blocks an action, the agent must find another approach, and after repeated blocks it stops and asks the user for permission.
A false positive is therefore costly: it blocks a harmless action, and repeated false positives halt autonomous work until the user responds.
In addition, the monitor runs before each reviewed action, so its latency delays the action and its inference cost adds to the cost of the agent.
A monitor in production thus needs a low false-positive rate, low latency and low cost.

\paragraph{Trained action-only monitors.}
\citet{sinha2026training} train small open-weight models as monitors, and our work builds on their setup.
Their monitors are \emph{action-only}: the monitor reads the visible trajectory of the agent, which consists of the user messages, the messages of the agent and the tool outputs.
It does not read the private reasoning of the agent or its activations.
The training set has 8,688 such trajectories from five sources, half of them benign and half with harmful behavior such as sabotage or data exfiltration.
To create training targets, a teacher model (Gemini 2.5 Pro) reads each trajectory together with a detailed specification of scheming behavior.
The teacher writes four candidate rationales, each of which ends in a suspicion score from 0 to 10.
A judge model (Claude Sonnet 4.5) then selects the best candidate, and the monitor learns to reproduce it through supervised fine-tuning (SFT).
For some monitors, a reinforcement learning (RL) stage follows, which rewards calibrated and correctly ranked scores.
At inference, the trained monitor therefore writes a rationale before it gives its score.

\paragraph{Evaluation suite and metric.}
Sinha et al.\ evaluate their monitors on six benchmarks that are out-of-distribution (OOD) for the training set: AgentDojo, CUA-SHADE-Arena, IaC, MLE-Sabotage, Rogue Eval and SHADE-Arena.
They drop every trajectory with more than 28,000 tokens, so that it fits the 32,000-token context of their shorter-context models.
Their metric, taken from \citet{storf2026constitutional}, is pAUROC@20: the area under the ROC curve for false-positive rates up to 20\%, normalized so that a perfect monitor scores 1.
They choose it because low false-positive rates are essential for deployment and for audits by human reviewers.
They compute it for each benchmark and report the unweighted mean, which they call Mean-OOD.
They pair this score with the inference cost in USD per 1,000 evaluations, at list prices without caching discounts.
On the resulting cost--performance frontier, their trained Qwen3.5-27B scores 0.831 at \$2.78, and Claude Opus 4.6 as a prompted monitor scores 0.942 at 34 times the cost.
We reuse their training trajectories, OOD suite, metric and cost axis, and we compare against their reported scores.
We change the output of the monitor and its training target (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
