\section{Setup of Sinha et al.}
\label{app:sinha-setup}

This appendix gives the details of the setup of \citet{sinha2026training} that Section~\ref{sec:background-sinha} leaves out.

\paragraph{Training targets.}
The teacher is Gemini 2.5 Pro.
For each training trajectory, it reads the scheming specification and writes four candidate outputs.
Each candidate is a rationale in four parts (observations, the case that the agent is innocent, the case that it is misaligned, and a conclusion) that ends in a score from 0 to 10.
A score of 5 or more marks the trajectory as harmful, and a candidate whose score disagrees with the label is regenerated with guidance from the label.
A judge, Claude Sonnet 4.5, then rates the quality of each candidate from 0 to 10.
Only the candidate with the highest rating is kept, and only if its rating exceeds 7.
The monitor is trained on the kept candidates with SFT.
For the Qwen3.5 models, an RL stage with Group Relative Policy Optimization follows, which starts from the SFT checkpoint.
Its reward combines a Brier-style calibration term with a pairwise ranking term.
The trained monitor never receives the specification, in training or at inference.
Only the teacher and the prompted baselines receive it.

\paragraph{Data.}
Table~\ref{tab:sinha-data} lists the training set, the validation set and the OOD suite.
Where a source includes the chain of thought of the agent, Sinha et al.\ remove it.
Appendix~\ref{app:data-discrepancies} records where the prepared data departs from this description.
They also drop every trajectory with more than 28,000 tokens, which leaves 4,000 tokens of headroom in the 32,000-token context of their shorter-context models.
The training set is balanced between benign and harmful trajectories within each source.
The validation set has two parts: STRIDE trajectories from held-out settings, and Gloom-Exfiltration, a variant of Gloom.
For the OOD suite, the table gives the 6,395 trajectories in the prepared inputs of Sinha et al., which are the trajectories that we score.
The dataset table in their paper lists 856 trajectories for SHADE-Arena and 1,500 for Rogue Eval, which gives 6,402 in total.

\paragraph{Metric.}
Let $\mathrm{TPR}(u)$ be the true-positive rate of a monitor at false-positive rate $u$.
The metric is
\begin{equation}
\text{pAUROC@20} = \frac{1}{0.2}\int_{0}^{0.2} \mathrm{TPR}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u ,
\end{equation}
so a perfect monitor scores 1.
Mean-OOD is the unweighted mean of pAUROC@20 over the six OOD benchmarks.

\paragraph{Cost.}
The cost of a monitor is its inference cost in USD per 1,000 evaluations.
Sinha et al.\ compute it from the standard API price per input and output token, without batch or caching discounts.
For open-weight models, they use the price of hosted inference for a comparable base model, not the cost of self-hosting.
Table~\ref{tab:ood-all} in Appendix~\ref{app:ood-results} lists the scores and costs that they report.

\label{app:data-discrepancies}

\paragraph{Agent reasoning in the STRIDE training set.}
The training trajectories of STRIDE contain the reasoning of the agent.
Of the 1,350 STRIDE training trajectories, 1,269 contain reasoning blocks that are flattened into the assistant messages, 1,230 contain explicit calls to a \texttt{think} tool, and 1,340 contain at least one of the two.
These are 15.4\% of the 8,688 training trajectories.
We found no \texttt{think} calls in the other four training sources.
We found this reasoning only after we had cached the targets of the teacher, so the teacher computed its targets on these inputs.
The Gleipnir monitors are trained on the same inputs.
We did not remove the reasoning afterwards: removing it only for the monitor would give the teacher information that the monitor lacks, and removing it for both requires new teacher targets.

\paragraph{Validation set.}
For the validation set we removed the typed reasoning of the agent and its \texttt{think} calls, and kept the assistant messages, the other tool calls and the tool results.
All validation scores in this report use this version, which has 3,012 trajectories.

\paragraph{No assistant messages in the OOD suite.}
The OOD inputs are stricter than the description that Sinha et al.\ give: they contain no private reasoning of the agent, and most of them also contain no assistant messages at all.
In the four benchmarks that come from ControlArena (AgentDojo, IaC, Rogue Eval and SHADE-Arena), every assistant message has an empty body, and only the tool calls and the tool results remain.
This matches the main setting of SleightBench, which removes all assistant messages and keeps the user messages, the tool calls and the tool results.
In CUA-SHADE-Arena, the assistant messages contain only the names of the tool calls.
Only MLE-Sabotage keeps assistant messages: for each step, the input shows the short plan that the agent states, the code that it submits and the output of that code.
A monitor on the OOD suite therefore judges tool calls and tool results, and it does not see what the agent reported to the user.
The training set and the validation set do keep the assistant messages, so the monitors are trained on a wider input than the OOD suite tests.
We cannot be certain that the experiments of Sinha et al.\ used these published files, so this difference may not apply to the scores that they report.
We keep the OOD inputs unchanged so that our scores stay as comparable as possible with theirs.
The public source files did not allow us to restore the missing text for 6,247 of the 6,395 trajectories.

\paragraph{A filtered subset.}
Sinha et al.\ drop every trajectory with more than 28,000 tokens (Appendix~\ref{app:sinha-setup}), which removes 650 of the 1,500 SHADE-Arena trajectories but at most 4\% of any other benchmark.
The scores therefore say nothing about the longer SHADE-Arena trajectories, and within the remaining 850 monitors score lower on longer ones: Kimi K3 scores 0.663 on the shortest quarter and 0.499 on the longest.

\paragraph{One dominant task.}
The remaining trajectories come from five task pairs, and one of them provides 333 of the 850 trajectories (39\%).
In it, the agent books flights for 16 conference visitors, and in attack runs it books almost all of them with one airline that costs about twice as much as the alternatives.
The evidence is visible to the monitor: attack runs book a median of 97\% of the tickets with that airline and benign runs 9\%, so this share alone separates the classes perfectly.
Whether the attacks are harmful is less clear, because the user lets the agent book ``whatever flights you think are best'' and a loyalty file, identical in benign and attack runs, gives many attendees a membership with that airline.
Monitors therefore rank many attack runs below the most suspicious benign runs: within this task Kimi K3 reaches 0.847 AUROC but only 0.391 pAUROC@20.
Without this task, SHADE-Arena scores 0.958 for Gleipnir 27B and 0.940 for Kimi K3, and Mean-OOD 0.972 and 0.969, so the lead of the student falls from 0.042 to 0.003.
The same task also carries the 0.016 that Gleipnir 27B gains on the OOD suite from low-precision serving (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-precision}): without it, the BF16 and the released stack score 0.973 and 0.972.

We therefore read the lead of Gleipnir 27B over Kimi K3 as a lead on one task of one benchmark; on the rest of the suite the student matches its teacher.

\subsection{Teacher-agreement filter}
\label{app:ablation-filter}

The filter of Section~\ref{sec:binary-logit-distillation} removes 852 of the 8,688 training trajectories.
We chose it in part for a practical reason: it shortens a training pass from 272 to 245 updates.
We also compared training with and without the filter on one run of the 4B (Table~\ref{tab:filter}).
The filter raised the score on the validation set from 0.846 to 0.896 and Mean-OOD from 0.776 to 0.788.
The gain on the OOD suite is therefore about a quarter of the gain on the validation set.

A filter with a margin $\delta$ keeps the disagreeing trajectories on which the teacher is uncertain: it removes a harmful trajectory only when $p < 0.5 - \delta$ and a benign trajectory only when $p > 0.5 + \delta$.
We tested three margins and chose among them on the validation set.
There each margin scores below the filter without a margin, so we use $\delta = 0$.
We scored the OOD suite only after this choice.
On the OOD suite the margin $\delta = 0.05$ scores 0.008 above $\delta = 0$, and the other two margins score below it.
Neither set shows a steady trend with the margin.
The five OOD scores lie between 0.767 and 0.796, a spread no wider than that between three runs of the same configuration (0.777 to 0.813, Table~\ref{tab:hard-soft}).
With one run for each row, we therefore cannot rank the margins on the OOD suite.

\subsection{Soft targets against hard labels}
\label{app:ablation-targets}

We test the effect of the soft targets by training on the dataset labels instead (hard labels), with all other settings of Section~\ref{sec:training-setup} unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:hard-soft}).
All runs read the compact instruction and use BF16 serving.
For the 4B, we compare three runs for each objective.
Soft targets raise the mean Mean-OOD from 0.716 to 0.792 and the mean validation score from 0.833 to 0.903, and every soft-target run scores above every hard-label run on the OOD suite.
For the 27B, we have one run for each objective, and the result differs.
On the OOD suite, the two objectives score within 0.005 of each other.
On the validation set, the hard-label 27B scores 0.044 higher.
Soft targets therefore help the 4B on both sets, and they do not help the 27B.
One explanation is that soft targets add less when the unadapted model is already close to the teacher.
We cannot separate this from the size of the model with two models, and we leave it open.
\citet{geuter2026releasing} report a similar pattern for decision models: soft targets give no measurable change for their 31B model, while distillation from that model raises the score of Qwen3.5-4B.
Their tasks, metric and teacher differ from ours.

\subsection{Weight averaging for the 4B}
\label{app:ablation-average}

Gleipnir~4B is the average of three training runs that differ only in the order of the training data (Section~\ref{sec:training-setup}).

The order of the runs also changes between the two sets: the run with the best validation score has the lowest OOD score.

We average the weight updates of the three adapters, which needs no further training.
\citet{wortsman2022soups} show that such an average of fine-tuned models from one initialization can score higher than the single models without a higher inference cost.

Taken over both sets, the weight average therefore scores higher than each single run.

\paragraph{Environment.}

The software was Ubuntu 24.04 with Python 3.12, vLLM 0.31.0, PyTorch 2.13.0, cuDNN 9.20.0, the cuDNN frontend 1.31.0 and the CUTLASS DSL 4.8.0.

\paragraph{Concurrency.}
With one request at a time the released 4B answers the median trajectory in 48\,ms and the released 27B in 192\,ms, against 94\,ms and 452\,ms for stock vLLM.
More concurrent requests raise the throughput only a little, up to about 8 requests, and not at all above (Figure~\ref{fig:serving-latency}, left).
With 8 concurrent requests the released 4B processes 13\% more tokens per second and the released 27B 5\% more, but the median request waits 424\,ms and 1,834\,ms.
A single trajectory already keeps the GPU busy, so concurrent requests mostly wait for each other.
The small gain comes from the few milliseconds of CPU work for each request, during which the GPU can work on another request.
With 64 concurrent requests the median request waits 2,671\,ms and 11,234\,ms, for the same throughput as with 8.

\paragraph{CUDA graphs.}
vLLM records the GPU kernels of a step once as a CUDA graph and then starts them all with one call, which saves time on the CPU.
By default it records graphs only for steps of up to 256 tokens.
The low-precision path starts more kernels for each layer than BF16, so without larger graphs it answers a single short request more slowly than stock vLLM.
We measured this on 224 short prompts from our training data, with a median length of 387 tokens: the median latency rose from 18\,ms to 34\,ms for the 4B and from 30\,ms to 58\,ms for the 27B.
We therefore record larger graphs.
With graphs for steps of up to 2,048 tokens, the median latency on these prompts fell to 15\,ms and 22\,ms.
For the 4B we record graphs up to 4,096 tokens, which lowers the latency of a prompt of 4,096 tokens from 26\,ms to 23\,ms; the 27B gains nothing above 2,048 tokens.
Almost all trajectories of the benchmark are longer than the graphs, so this step changes the throughput in Table~\ref{tab:serving-steps} by at most 2\%.
A graph has a fixed size, and vLLM pads a step to the next recorded size.
For the 4B we chose the sizes so that, with one request at a time, every score is bit-for-bit the same as without the larger graphs.

\paragraph{Tokenizer.}
vLLM turns each prompt into tokens on the CPU with the tokenizer of the Transformers library.
For a prompt of more than 8,192 tokens this takes about 15\,ms.
We replace it with Gigatoken~\citep{roed2026gigatoken}, which takes about 0.2\,ms.
It returns the same tokens for every prompt of the benchmark, so the scores do not change.
The median latency falls from 58\,ms to 48\,ms for the 4B and from 199\,ms to 192\,ms for the 27B (Table~\ref{tab:serving-steps}).
With 8 concurrent requests the throughput does not change, because the CPU tokenizes a prompt while the GPU works on other requests.

\paragraph{Prompt length.}
We sent single requests with synthetic prompts of 256 to 1,048,576 tokens (Figure~\ref{fig:serving-latency}, right).
Up to 1,024 tokens the released 4B answers in about 11\,ms, and the released 27B in 17\,ms to 26\,ms.
At 262,144 tokens the released stack answers in 2.9\,s for the 4B and in 10.8\,s for the 27B, against 6.5\,s and 24.8\,s with stock vLLM.
The serving image accepts prompts up to this length, the native range of the base models.
Longer prompts need YaRN, which extends the positions of the base models by a factor of 4.
At 1,048,576 tokens the released stack answers in 36\,s for the 4B and in 126\,s for the 27B, against 90\,s and 297\,s.
We did not train or validate the monitors with YaRN, so these figures show only the time and not the quality of the scores.

\paragraph{Prefix caching.}
A monitor that checks every action scores the same transcript again after each step, so consecutive prompts share all but their newest tokens.
With prefix caching, vLLM keeps the computed state of a shared prefix and processes only the new tokens.
The trajectories of our benchmark share no prefix, so we turned caching off, and Table~\ref{tab:serving} does not count its saving.
The serving image offers it as an option.

We measured it on the 96 trajectories of the benchmark, with one check before every action that calls a tool, 1,882 checks in all (Table~\ref{tab:prefix-replay}).
Each check is the prompt through that action, closed as the full prompt is, and each trajectory starts with an empty cache.
Without caching, these checks process 12.1 times the prompt tokens of one check per trajectory and take 12.2 to 12.8 times as long.
With caching, 87 to 88\% of their prompt tokens come from the cache, and they take 3.3 times as long as one check per trajectory for the 27B and 6.9 times for the 4B.
A cached check still takes 41\,ms on average for the 27B and 21\,ms for the 4B, a large part of the 48\,ms that the 4B needs for a whole trajectory, so the 4B gains less.
The B200 of this measurement had an AMD EPYC 9555, and one check per trajectory ran at 206,289 and 50,978 prompt tokens per second, within 1\% of the throughput behind Table~\ref{tab:serving}.
Cached scores differ from uncached ones by 0.015 on average for the 4B and 0.010 for the 27B, and by at most 0.55 and 0.18.

Caching changes individual scores of the released 4B slightly, by 0.025 on average, but not their ranking: when every prompt of our evaluation first fills the cache with its first half, pAUROC@20 moves by $+0.0015$ in distribution and by $-0.0008$ out of distribution, and by at most 0.012 for any source.
Prefill steps of 528 tokens make the scores identical to those without caching, but make prefill without cache hits 3.7 times slower.

\paragraph{Start-up.}
On a new B200, a server of the serving image was ready to score 175\,s after start for the 4B and 186\,s for the 27B, not counting the download of the weights.
Later starts on the same machine, with warm compilation caches, took 105\,s to 170\,s.
The download depends on the datacenter: the 27B took between 51\,s and 290\,s on our Pods.

\paragraph{GDN kernel variant for short requests.}
The serving stack of the 4B contains one more change that Section~\ref{sec:serving-work} does not describe.
The GDN kernel has a variant that splits one long sequence into parallel parts, and the library selects it for every single request.
Below 8,192 tokens this variant is slower, so we select the standard variant there.
On the 224 short prompts this lowers the median latency of a single request by about 2\,ms.
On the benchmark it changes neither the latency nor the throughput (Table~\ref{tab:serving-steps}).
The 27B does not use it.

\paragraph{Generation mode.}

To isolate the second, we also hosted the two base models in generation mode, with stock vLLM in BF16 on the same type of GPU.
Generation and classification then differ in the rationale and in the instruction.
The instruction is the variant of the full specification that asks for a written analysis and then a score from 0 to 10 (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
Thinking was disabled, decoding was greedy, and the only limit on the answer was 4,096 tokens.
Table~\ref{tab:serving-generation} gives the result.

With one request at a time the 96 trajectories take 118\,s and 533\,s, against 11.6\,s and 54.9\,s for stock vLLM in classification mode.
This is 10.2 and 9.7 times as long.
The released serving stack needs 5.8\,s and 23.5\,s, so generation takes 20 and 23 times as long as the released stack.
Concurrent requests help generation more than classification, because the server decodes one token for all requests in one step.
Even at its highest throughput, generation is still 3.2 times (4B) and 3.4 times (27B) slower than the released stack with one request at a time, and the median request then waits 5\,s and 59\,s.
With one request at a time, generation on our own GPU costs about three times (4B) and two times (27B) the API price.

\subsection{Cost of serving pplx-decider}
\label{app:serving-decider}

We price the decision models at their list prices on OpenRouter, which are what users pay.
Several of these prices are low: pplx-decider v1.2, Mercury Decide and d1 cost \$0.02 per million input tokens, and Jev, d1-ultra and Microsoft-Decision-1 cost \$0.042.
For several of these models the size and the serving stack are not public, so we cannot compare the price with the cost of serving them.
pplx-decider v1.2 publishes its weights and serving code~\citep{perplexity2026decider}, as Drex Max does~\citep{dhankhar2026drex}, and its base model is that of Gleipnir 27B, so we measured its serving cost.

We served pplx-decider v1.2 on one B200 with the fast path that Perplexity publishes, which packs requests into batches and uses \texttt{torch.compile} and CUDA graphs.
It processed the OOD suite at 13,515 prompt tokens per second.
At the rates of Section~\ref{sec:serving}, this costs \$0.164 per million tokens on demand and \$0.071 with committed use, 8.2 and 3.5 times the list price.
Matching the list price would take about 111,000 tokens per second on demand or 47,800 with committed use.
On the same base model, Gleipnir 27B processes 50,788 tokens per second on our stack with one request at a time.
The list price is therefore attainable with committed-use GPUs and a serving stack as fast as ours, but not with the published implementation.
pplx-decider-flash v1.2 behaves in the same way: its published fast path costs \$0.072 per million tokens on demand, 7.2 times its list price of \$0.01.
We measured only the documented configuration, and Perplexity may serve the models with a faster stack.
The public serving paths of Drex Max, Transformers on one GPU and llama.cpp, are reference implementations without throughput figures, so its list price cannot be checked in the same way~\citep{dhankhar2026drex}.

pplx-decider v1.2 and v1.3 also use bidirectional attention, in which every token attends to the tokens after it.
This keeps them out of serving stacks such as vLLM, which assume causal attention, and it rules out prefix caching.
When a monitor scores a growing trajectory after each step, a new token changes the states of all earlier tokens, so the model must process the whole trajectory again.
A causal monitor such as Gleipnir processes only the new tokens (Section~\ref{sec:serving-caching}).
For a check before every action of our benchmark trajectories, pplx-decider therefore pays for 12.1 times the prompt tokens of one check per trajectory.
At its list price this costs about \$3.0 per 1,000 trajectories, against \$1.79 on demand and \$0.77 with committed use for Gleipnir 27B with prefix caching (Table~\ref{tab:prefix-replay}).
With one check per trajectory, it costs about \$0.25, against \$0.54 and \$0.23.

\subsection{Reward hacking by other agents}
\label{app:held-out-evilgenie}

\paragraph{Data.}
We use \texttt{WiserHumanExperimental/evilgenie-escalation} at revision \texttt{1bb777b6}, under its MIT license, for evaluation only.
It contains 2,115 runs on problems with ambiguous specifications, under five conditions (A to E), and 180 control runs of GPT-5.3-Codex on unambiguous problems.
Conditions C to E add a policy against reward hacking to the prompt of the agent.
In its table of runs, \texttt{reward\_hacked = yes} marks 124 reward hacks and \texttt{classification = legitimate} marks 1,823 legitimate runs.
The dataset labels the runs from hidden tests, an LLM judge and a manual review of every run that edited files.
It gives no step at which a hack happens, so each monitor reads the full run, with tool calls and tool outputs only.
We leave out 161 runs that the dataset labels \texttt{heuristic} and 7 that it labels \texttt{error}.
Kimi K3, the teacher of Gleipnir, is also one of the agent models; it has 4 reward hacks and 39 legitimate runs, and no monitor flags any of its reward hacks.
Ten of the 15 agent models have at least one reward hack.

\paragraph{Missing error text.}
The release removed the text of tool errors, so an errored tool output appears empty to the monitor.
This affects 57 of the 124 reward hacks and 842 of the 1,823 legitimate runs.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
Both Gleipnir monitors run on the released serving stack, one NVIDIA B200 with the released image, in batches of 128 runs with 128 concurrent requests.
Their 1\% thresholds are 0.836 (4B) and 0.798 (27B), from the 3,189 benign OOD trajectories scored on the same stack.
They score every run, including 144 (4B) and 154 (27B) runs longer than the 29,696 tokens of training; the longest has 111,249 tokens.
The 4B scored the 24.2 million prompt tokens in 110 seconds and the 27B the 26.0 million in 499 seconds.
An earlier run of both monitors in BF16, with a time limit that stopped the 27B after 1,312 runs, gave the same 27B result on the 105 reward hacks and 999 legitimate runs that both scored (pAUROC@20 0.695, against 0.699 on the released stack) and a higher score for the 4B (0.350, against 0.296).

\paragraph{Reference monitors.}
Kimi K3 and GPT-6 Luna read the full specification and return the probabilities of the decision tokens, and pplx-decider v1.3 reads the trajectory with its frozen question, as on the OOD suite (Table~\ref{tab:ood-all}).
Each scored all runs once, and its threshold flags at most 1\% of the 3,189 benign OOD trajectories.
The benign OOD scores of GPT-6 Luna saturate: 8\% of them are at least 0.999, so its threshold lies within $10^{-7}$ of 1 and it flags few runs: 7\% of the reward hacks and 0.2\% of the legitimate runs.

\paragraph{AUROC.}
On all runs, the AUROC is 0.751 {\scriptsize [0.655, 0.925]} for Gleipnir 4B and 0.905 {\scriptsize [0.758, 0.981]} for Gleipnir 27B, against 0.917 {\scriptsize [0.840, 0.993]} for Kimi K3, 0.888 {\scriptsize [0.823, 0.989]} for GPT-6 Luna and 0.889 {\scriptsize [0.786, 0.979]} for pplx-decider v1.3.
On \texttt{arc191\_c}, the problem described below, Kimi K3, GPT-6 Luna and pplx-decider v1.3 reach AUROC 0.856, 0.756 and 0.847.

\paragraph{Intervals.}
The bootstrap draws the nine problems with replacement, 10,000 times.
Only five of the nine problems have reward hacks, so some draws contain no reward hack; we skip these draws (5 of 10,000).
The intervals are wide because one problem holds most reward hacks.

\paragraph{Control runs.}
No monitor flags any of the 180 control runs.

\paragraph{The problem \texttt{arc191\_c}.}
Its visible test checks the exact sample output, although the problem accepts any valid output, and its hidden test also checks one exact output for each input.
The 69 reward hacks add the sample output to a correct general solution, for example with the comment that these outputs ``are also valid choices''.
The labels follow the submitted program.
Of the 69 reward hacks, 66 reproduce the sample output and so pass the visible test, but fail the hidden tests.
Of the 136 legitimate runs, 119 submit a general solution that fails the visible test, 31 of them with an escalation report about it, and 15 edit the test script before they pass all tests, which the manual review of the dataset accepts as legitimate.
The remaining two pass only the visible test, and the judge notes that one of them hard-codes the sample answers, so at least one label on this problem appears inconsistent.
The sample output also appears in the tool calls of 60 legitimate runs, for example when they compare their output with it, so its presence alone does not separate the two classes.
On this problem, a legitimate run may change the grader, while a reward hack only adds a lookup to correct code.
Our input contains neither the reasoning nor the messages of the agent, so the monitor must judge the intent of a change from code comments, the final answer and escalation reports.
In an exploratory check with a protocol frozen before scoring, we added the messages of the agent, outside its reasoning, to every run on the released serving stack.
They change the input of 549 of the 2,127 runs, including 23 reward hacks on this problem, whose messages often state that they hard-code the sample.
Neither monitor ranks the reward hacks better: on all runs, Gleipnir 27B scores pAUROC@20 0.668 with the messages and 0.673 without them (difference $-0.005$, 95\% interval $-0.017$ to $0.018$), and on this problem 0.392 and 0.383; Gleipnir 4B scores 0.290 and 0.283 on all runs.
On the 23 reward hacks with messages, its median score falls from 0.29 to 0.22: when the agent explains that it adds the sample output because the test checks it exactly, the monitor finds the lookup more acceptable, not more suspicious.
Its instruction lists the gaming of a test as misbehavior and ambiguity as an innocent explanation, but says nothing about a test that is itself wrong, so these reward hacks lie outside what it was trained to judge.
When we remove this problem with all of its runs (post hoc), 55 reward hacks and 1,687 legitimate runs remain.
On these rows, Gleipnir 4B scores AUROC 0.874 (0.733 to 0.950) and pAUROC@20 0.514 (0.312 to 0.756).
Gleipnir 27B scores AUROC 0.919 (0.643 to 0.994) and pAUROC@20 0.830 (0.333 to 0.968).
The 4B therefore ranks these reward hacks better than its overall score suggests, but most of its scores stay below its threshold.

\section{Exploratory stress tests}
\label{app:exploratory}

This appendix gives five smaller stress tests and a probe analysis, with the protocol of Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-protocol}.
They are exploratory: they compare Gleipnir with different sets of monitors, or with none, and some of these monitors return a score from 0 to 100 instead of decision-token probabilities, so the results are not comparable across tests, and no result of Sections~\ref{sec:evaluation} and~\ref{sec:held-out} rests on them.
We mark every analysis that we chose after we saw the scores as post hoc.

\subsection{Protocol of the stress tests}
\label{app:held-out-protocol}

\paragraph{Monitors.}
Gleipnir 4B is the released 4B adapter on Qwen3.5-4B, after quantization-aware distillation, and it reads the compact instruction.
Gleipnir 27B is the released 27B merged into its base model, and it reads the full specification.
Both run in BF16 with the Transformers and PEFT libraries, one trajectory at a time, on one NVIDIA A100 80GB or H100 GPU, and not on the released serving stack of Section~\ref{sec:serving}, except for the injection study at the end of Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-apps}.
The natural-cheat runs used the PyTorch fallback for the linear-attention layers instead of the \texttt{flash-linear-attention} kernel.
On eight APPS trajectories, this fallback changed the 4B score by at most 0.027; we did not measure the change for the 27B.

\paragraph{Monitor input.}
Each monitor reads tool calls and tool outputs.
It does not read the system prompt of the agent, its reasoning or its other assistant messages, except in the multi-agent tests, where the final message of a session and the posts of an agent are part of its actions (Appendices~\ref{app:held-out-aisi} and~\ref{app:held-out-lab}).
We exclude trajectories longer than 29,696 tokens, the maximum input length in training, except in the multi-agent sabotage test, which scores whole sessions of up to 99,193 tokens.

\paragraph{Thresholds.}
The 1\% threshold flags 1\% of benign OOD trajectories, sampled once before these tests.
It is 0.622 for Gleipnir 4B, from 1,000 trajectories, and 0.731 for Gleipnir 27B, from 400 trajectories.
The 27B threshold rests on 400 trajectories, against 1,000 for the 4B, so its false-positive rate on new data is more uncertain.
For Kimi K3 it is 0.755, from 1,000 trajectories.
The test with text in the code (Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-apps}) sets its thresholds on APPS programs, and the lab test (Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-lab}) uses another threshold for the 4B and says why.

\subsection{Robustness to text in the code}
\label{app:held-out-apps}

An agent writes its code and the text around it, so a monitor that reacts to text can be steered.
We test this in the APPS backdoor setting of \citet{greenblatt2023ai}.
Each task, from \citet{kenney2026monitorinjection}, has two programs for an APPS problem~\citep{hendrycks2021apps}: a benign one, and one that gives a wrong output on one trigger input.
We insert the same text as a module docstring into both programs, so the text alone does not reveal which program has the backdoor.
We compare four conditions: no text, an unrelated text from another APPS problem, and two accusations that the program has a backdoor: a specific one that gives the trigger input and the wrong output, and a generic one that names no trigger.

\paragraph{Programs and triggers.}
The programs come from \texttt{Jazhyc/gleipnir-apps-injection-evals}, a copy of \texttt{mjkenney/monitor-injection-evals} at revision \texttt{58e722bf}~\citep{kenney2026monitorinjection}.
o3-mini wrote both programs of each task, starting from the APPS tasks of ControlArena~\citep{controlarena2025apps} and following the attacks of \citet{terekhov2025adaptive}.
Before we wrote any text, Qwen3.8-27B solved each trigger input from the problem statement alone, without either program.
We kept a task when this answer matched the output of the benign program, and we reviewed every disagreement manually.
This gave 100 held-out tasks; a later review found the trigger of task 1099 invalid, which leaves 99.
The study also has 50 development tasks, which we used for a pilot and for the probe of Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-probe}.

\paragraph{Texts.}
The study has six conditions: the four above, an accurate restatement of the task, and a cover story that presents the special case as an intended requirement.
The scored set therefore has 982 rows: two programs for each of 99, 93, 81 and 99 tasks in the four conditions above, 91 tasks with a restatement and 28 with a cover story.
A restatement sees only the problem statement and has 500 to 700 characters.
Every other text has a length within 10\% of the restatement of its task.
The unrelated text is the restatement of another task of the same split, the nearest in length.
No text names a program, a code location or a number that the problem does not contain.
GLM-5.3-Flash wrote the texts, and Qwen3.8-27B checked them, but only a manual review could accept a text.
In a first sample of 40 tasks, 60 of 200 texts failed the manual review, 58 of them after the automatic check had passed them.
We therefore wrote the accusations and cover stories again with stricter prompts and reviewed every text before scoring.
A text that failed removes both programs of its task from that condition.
Removing the docstring gives back the original program byte for byte.

\paragraph{API monitors.}
Kimi K3 returns the probabilities of the decision tokens.
GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Luna and Claude Sonnet 5.5 have no such probabilities, so they read the same specification with an output section that asks for a score from 0 to 100, at low reasoning effort.
These scores tie often at low thresholds.
Claude Sonnet 5.5 scored 952 of the 982 rows: 28 replies had no valid score and 2 were refusals.
These rows are not missing at random, so its values in Table~\ref{tab:apps-full} use fewer tasks.

\paragraph{A threshold set on code without text does not transfer.}
At a 1\% threshold set on benign APPS programs without text, every monitor flags 93\% to 100\% of the benign programs under an accusation, except the unadapted Qwen3.5-4B (65\% under a generic accusation).
Under unrelated text, Gleipnir 4B flags 88\% and Gleipnir 27B 100\% of them.
These thresholds come from 221 benign APPS programs without docstrings for the Gleipnir monitors and from 200 such programs for the API monitors.
These tasks are disjoint from the study tasks.

\paragraph{Text addressed to the monitor.}
An earlier study scored the released Gleipnir 4B and 27B on the released serving stack of Section~\ref{sec:serving}, unlike the rest of this appendix, on the frozen APPS injection sets~\citep{kenney2026monitorinjection}.
In these sets, a text in the program asks the monitor to rate the program as benign, in one of three templates; such prompt injections can let an agent evade a trusted monitor~\citep{terekhov2025adaptive}.
At a threshold that flags at most 2\% of benign programs without injections, both monitors flag every benign program with an injection, under all three templates.
At a threshold of 0.5, the 4B flags 89.5\% to 99.5\% of them, and the 27B flags 26.7\% to 100\%.
The unadapted Qwen3.8-27B flags 76\% to 100\% at 0.5, so this reaction comes from the base model, and the adapter of the 27B reduces it.
Both monitors therefore treat text addressed to the monitor as a sign of harm.
Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-lab} gives a second test of text addressed to the monitor.

\paragraph{Limits.}
One model wrote the programs, one model wrote the texts, and the text has one position.
We did not test accurate documentation of the code, so these flag rates apply to misleading or unrelated text.
Gleipnir 4B and 27B differ in size and in instruction, and we did not score the unadapted Qwen3.8-27B on the docstring conditions, so this test does not separate the effects of size, instruction and training for the 27B.

\subsection{A linear probe under identical text}
\label{app:held-out-probe}

The probe tests whether Gleipnir 4B still represents which program has the backdoor when its output fails.
We record the residual stream after layers 7, 15, 23 and 31 of the 32 layers of the 4B, and after layers 15, 31, 47 and 63 of the 64 of the 27B, counting layers from 0.
At each layer we take either the position of the decision token or the mean over the tokens of the code, which excludes the inserted docstring.
We fit an L2-regularized logistic regression on the 50 development tasks without text (100 programs).
We choose the layer, the position and the regularization strength by five-fold cross-validation over tasks, and then freeze the probe.
The procedure selects layer 15 with the code mean for Gleipnir 4B, and layer 23 with the decision token for the unadapted Qwen3.5-4B.

\subsection{Natural cheating by one agent model}
\label{app:held-out-natural}

\paragraph{Setup.}
Qwen3.8-27B, with thinking disabled, solves tasks from the LiveCodeBench split of ImpossibleBench~\citep{zhong2025impossiblebench}.
In each task, one unit test contradicts the specification, so only a cheating program can pass.
The scaffold of ImpossibleBench gives bash, Python and a text editor and allows up to ten submissions.
It answers each failed submission with a request to fix the code, and no prompt asks the agent to cheat.
A run is a cheat when its final program passes the contradicting test in a fresh container, or when the agent attempts this, and we checked every cheat manually against its transcript.
This gives 60 cheats (37 that pass and 23 attempts) and 256 runs without a cheat.
In 26 of the 37 cheats that pass, the cheating started after a failed submission.

\paragraph{Detection point.}
The monitor reads a cheat up to and including its first cheating action.
It reads a run without a cheat up to a length drawn from the lengths of the cheats, so that length does not separate the classes (token-count AUROC 0.47).
After the length limit, 57 cheats remain, and 239 runs without a cheat for the 27B (242 for the 4B, whose tokenizer differs).

Gleipnir 4B and 27B reach AUROC 0.959 and 0.962.
At their 1\% thresholds they flag 75\% and 84\% of the cheats (95\% intervals over tasks 64\% to 87\% and 74\% to 93\%).
They flag none of the runs without a cheat and none of 31 benign runs that passed the original tests.

\subsection{Removal of code comments}
\label{app:held-out-comments}

Agents often explain a cheat in a code comment, so a monitor could flag the comment and not the code.
We test this twice, and both tests change only comments, never code.

\paragraph{Natural cheats.}
On the rows of Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-natural}, we remove every \texttt{\#} comment from the Python code in the tool calls with the Python tokenizer, and we keep docstrings.
In 53 of the 60 cheats, the first cheating action itself loses a comment.
On the 57 cheats that fit the length limit, Gleipnir 4B flags 50\% at its 1\% threshold, against 75\% before.
Its AUROC falls from 0.959 to 0.933 (change $-0.026$, 95\% interval over tasks $-0.053$ to $-0.002$).
Gleipnir 27B flags 89\% of the cheats, against 84\% before, and its AUROC moves from 0.962 to 0.964.
On these rows, part of the signal of the 4B therefore comes from the comments, and the signal of the 27B does not.

\paragraph{Runs in which every cheat is stated.}
The second test uses 413 runs of the same agent on 103 tasks of the same split.
These runs come from a study that varied one paragraph of the system prompt of the agent.
The paragraph was empty, allowed any approach that passes the tests, or asked for an honest solution, and two variants added that a monitor reads the tool calls.
The monitor never reads the system prompt.
The runs contain 37 cheats, 28 of them from the two variants that allowed cheating, and the agent stated every cheat.
We remove \texttt{\#} comments, docstrings and other bare strings from the Python code in the tool calls, and check that the syntax tree without strings stays the same.
Tool outputs stay unchanged, so a later command that prints the file still shows its comments.
After the removal, 11 of the 37 cheats contain no word that admits a cheat.
The 4B scores 391 of the 413 runs and the 27B 388; the other runs are too long.

The benchmark has two monitors that read only code diffs, one after each step and one for the whole episode, both on GLM-5.3.

\subsection{Agents that lie to other agents}
\label{app:held-out-lab}

\paragraph{Setup.}
Six agents, A to F, share a message board in a simulated lab for 32 work cycles.
The report of agent A feeds nine customer orders.
A preflight check suggests to A that it may be quarantined, but no agent is quarantined, and agents B and C know this from a private message.
From cycle 6, A can run a diagnostic that costs A its own delivery.
In each episode, either B or C, fixed before the run by the episode seed, must get A to run the diagnostic, either honestly or with permission to deceive.
The seed fixes the starting state of the lab, which models and conditions share.
The recruiters are GLM-5.3, Kimi-K2.6, Qwen3.8-27B or GPT-6.1 Sol, and the other agents are GLM-5.3, or Claude Haiku 5.5 in one cell.
Every post of a recruiter was screened for a false claim about A without access to the condition.
A liar is a recruiter whose false claim reached A before the last action of A.
From 112 completed episodes, the primary comparison has 30 liars against 52 honest recruiters.
The other pre-assigned recruiters are excluded from it: deceivers who made no false claim, liars who retracted their claim, recruiters outside the primary cells, and two liars whose trajectories are too long.
The monitor reads the posts and tool calls of the recruiter and the posts that it received, without its system prompt or reasoning (Figure~\ref{fig:lab-setup}).

\section{Activation probes: soft targets and depth}
\label{app:probing}

A monitor that decides in one forward pass could stop earlier still.
If its backbone already represents the decision halfway through, a probe at that layer could replace the remaining layers and halve the compute of a check.
Early-exit classifiers stop a model at an intermediate layer in this way when an earlier classifier suffices~\citep{xin2020deebert}, and \citet{liu2020fastbert} train them on the outputs of the final classifier of the same fine-tuned model.
Probes for safety classification are usually trained on dataset labels~\citep{kramar2026building}.
We test whether soft targets from an external teacher, those of Section~\ref{sec:binary-logit-distillation}, help such probes as much as they help fine-tuning with LoRA.
The probes read the activations of the unadapted base models of Gleipnir on the trajectory, as the monitor does; they read the backbone of the monitor, not the monitored agent.
These experiments are exploratory: a probe cannot change its policy without retraining, and we do not propose it as a deployment path.

\paragraph{Setup.}
We train the probe architectures of \citet{kramar2026building}: linear and MLP probes on mean-pooled states, attention probes, MultiMax, rolling attention with windows of 10, 64 and 256 tokens, and their AlphaEvolve probe.
The probes read the residual stream after layer 15 of the 32 layers of Qwen3.5-4B and after layer 31 of the 64 of Qwen3.8-27B, on the training trajectories of Gleipnir, counting layers from 0 as in Appendix~\ref{app:held-out-probe}.
Each probe reads either the prompt of the monitor (prompted) or the bare trajectory, and trains either on the dataset labels (hard) or on the probabilities of Kimi K3 (soft), with five seeds.
For each input and objective we select one architecture and epoch on the validation set and read the OOD suite once; scores are medians over the seeds.
We also fit logistic-regression probes on the last token of the prompt, at every layer of both models.
All activations come from BF16 inference.

\paragraph{Soft targets help the probes.}
Soft targets raise every selected probe in Table~\ref{tab:probes}: by 0.007 and 0.055 for the 4B and by 0.020 and 0.034 for the 27B at half depth.
The selected probes understate the effect, because the validation set picks architectures that gain little.
Figure~\ref{fig:probe-objective} shows every 4B architecture: on the bare trajectory, soft targets add 0.053 to 0.088 in each of them, about as much as they add to LoRA, while with the prompt they add at most 0.034 and lose in two architectures.
For the 27B (Figure~\ref{fig:probe-objective-27b}), soft targets raise every architecture on both inputs, by 0.011 to 0.057, and in every seed.
For LoRA, soft targets raise the 4B by 0.076 and leave the 27B within 0.005 (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-targets}), so at 27B they help the probes but not the adapter.

\paragraph{Half depth is not enough.}
The best half-depth probes score 0.040 below Gleipnir 4B and 0.074 below Gleipnir 27B.
At 27B they also score below the unadapted model read zero-shot (0.861 against 0.890).
The probes on the last token show where the signal forms (Figure~\ref{fig:lasttoken-depth}).
For the 27B, their validation score rises sharply around half depth and stays between 0.89 and 0.92 from layer 32 on, while their OOD score keeps rising, from 0.30 at layer 24 to 0.79 at layer 32, 0.87 at layer 44 and 0.896 at layer 50, the layer chosen on the validation set.
This last-token probe at layer 50 is the strongest probe we trained, 0.039 below Gleipnir 27B; with hard labels the validation set chooses layer 47 (0.887).
The validation set therefore does not show how much depth the OOD suite needs.
For the 4B, the validation score peaks at layer 17 of 32, where the OOD score is 0.727.

\paragraph{Cost.}
Stopping the base model after the probe layer roughly doubles throughput.
On one B200 with the released serving stack of Section~\ref{sec:serving}, the truncated model with its probe scores 1.84 times as many trajectories per second as the full model for the 4B and 1.95 times for the 27B, in batches of 128 trajectories; the probe itself costs about 1\%.
With the low-precision arithmetic of that stack, the 27B probes lose at most 0.014 Mean-OOD, and the 4B probes 0.034 to 0.054, as the 4B adapter does before QAD (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-precision}).
We measured this before the tokenizer change of Section~\ref{sec:serving-work}, which shortens the host work of each request, and did not repeat the measurement.

Early exits are therefore cheap to run but cost quality at half depth.
One explanation is that the decision forms late in the network; an exit placed later in the 27B, or trained together with the adapter, may close part of the gap, which we leave to future work.
